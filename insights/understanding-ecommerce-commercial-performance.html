Understanding ecommerce commercial performance

By Collective

Ecommerce performance connects sales, costs, customer behaviour and the cash required to keep trading. Understanding it means examining how those parts work together.

Revenue shows the sales result. Contribution shows what remains after specified costs. Profit accounts for the further expenses included in the calculation. Cash determines whether the business can meet its commitments when they fall due.

An executive dashboard should make these relationships visible. Its purpose is to help you recognise what is changing, understand the commercial consequences and identify what needs investigation.

Begin with the business result

A channel report can show strong advertising returns while the business retains little profit. Revenue can increase while cash becomes tighter. A lower efficiency ratio can accompany a higher absolute profit.

None of these patterns is a complete diagnosis. Each is a reason to examine the connected figures.

Begin with three questions:

  1. What did the business earn?
  2. What remained after the relevant costs?
  3. What cash and stock commitments must it support next?

This establishes the context for reading marketing performance. Channel metrics then help explain how the business reaches and converts customers, rather than becoming the sole definition of success.

The commercial objective also needs to be clear. Increasing profit, acquiring more customers and preserving cash can place different demands on the business. The dashboard should reveal those trade-offs.

Follow revenue through to profit

The first relationship is the calculation from revenue to operating profit.

MeasureWhat it shows
Net revenueSales after the specified reductions, such as discounts and refunds, on a consistent tax basis
Gross profitNet revenue less the cost of goods sold
Contribution before marketingGross profit less the included fulfilment, transaction and other variable costs
Contribution after marketingThe amount remaining after media and other included marketing costs
Operating profitContribution after marketing less the remaining operating expenses

The definitions matter because reports sometimes use the same label for different calculations.

A contribution figure that excludes advertising cannot be compared directly with one that includes it. An operating-profit figure should not be called net profit if tax and other applicable items remain outside the calculation.

Every cost should be included once. Missing costs should remain visible rather than being treated as zero.

Read dollars and margins together

Profit dollars show the absolute result. Profit margin shows how much of each revenue dollar remains.

Example Commerce, the illustrative business used throughout this series, demonstrates why both readings matter. All figures below are in Australian dollars and exclude GST.

MeasureSeptember 2026October 2026
Net revenue$240,000$270,000
Contribution after marketing$64,000$67,500
Operating profit$38,000$40,500
Operating margin15.83%15.00%

The business produced $2,500 more operating profit in October, while its operating margin declined.

That is a more useful description than calling the month better or worse from one figure alone. The next question is which changes in sales and costs produced the result, and whether they are likely to persist.

Choose a comparison that fits the question

A current figure becomes more informative when compared with an appropriate reference.

The previous month shows recent movement. The corresponding month last year provides seasonal context. A longer period helps establish whether a pattern extends beyond one month.

These comparisons can produce different readings without contradicting each other.

Example Commerce’s October operating margin of 15.00% is below September’s 15.83%, but above the previous October’s 13.44%.

The recent comparison shows margin pressure. The annual comparison shows improvement against the corresponding period last year. Both belong in the assessment.

Before interpreting either, check:

  • Whether the periods have equivalent coverage.
  • Whether revenue and costs use consistent definitions.
  • Whether promotions, stock availability or trading conditions differed.
  • Whether the figures are complete or still subject to adjustment.

Also distinguish an absolute change from a relative change. A dollar increase in profit, a percentage increase in profit and a percentage-point change in margin describe different things.

The comparison establishes what moved. It does not establish the cause.

Connect marketing efficiency with profit

Marketing efficiency measures are useful when their scope is clear.

Marketing efficiency ratio, or MER, divides business revenue by paid media spend. It describes the relationship between the business’s total revenue and that spending.

Return on ad spend, or ROAS, divides attributed conversion value by the corresponding advertising spend. Its meaning depends on the platform’s conversion and attribution settings.

MER and ROAS therefore answer different questions.

A customer may interact with several channels before purchasing. More than one platform may receive credit. Adding those attributed values does not produce a reliable business revenue total.

MER avoids that particular addition, but it does not prove that advertising caused all the business’s revenue.

More profit can accompany lower efficiency

Example Commerce’s MER fell from 6.67 in September to 6.00 in October. Its operating profit nevertheless increased from $38,000 to $40,500.

The additional sales left enough contribution to cover the additional costs and increase operating profit.

This explains the financial relationship. It does not establish that additional advertising caused the increase in sales.

It also does not establish that another spending increase would produce the same response. Historical performance describes the activity already undertaken. Additional spending may reach different customers, incur different costs or change the product mix.

A growth scenario must therefore state its assumptions about additional revenue, contribution and costs. It should show what happens if the response is weaker than expected.

Relate acquisition cost to customer contribution

Customer acquisition cost is meaningful only when the customer population and included costs are defined.

A business-level media CAC divides period paid media spend by first-time customers purchasing in that period. It is a useful blended measure, but it does not precisely allocate spending to each acquired customer.

A cohort acquisition cost uses expenditure allocated to a defined group, such as customers first acquired in June. Its allocation method must be understood.

Both differ from an allowable CAC.

Allowable CAC is the acquisition amount the business can support under specified contribution and profit requirements. It is derived from economics, not simply selected because it appears achievable.

For a first-order assessment, begin with first-order revenue and its associated product, fulfilment and transaction costs. Then account for the remaining cost and profit requirements.

A higher average order value can increase the allowance when it produces more contribution. Discounting, product mix or delivery costs can reduce that benefit.

Customer value includes timing

Some customers contribute beyond their first purchase. That makes repeat purchasing relevant to acquisition economics, but the value needs a clear definition.

Revenue lifetime value describes revenue over a stated horizon. Contribution lifetime value deducts the costs included in its definition. They are not interchangeable.

Payback then asks how long cumulative contribution takes to recover the specified acquisition cost.

The June cohort in this series has an allocated acquisition media cost of $42 per customer. Its cumulative contribution before that acquisition cost reaches $42 at day 45 and $61 at day 90.

Under the illustrative record, acquisition media is recovered at day 45. By day 90, $19 per customer remains after that acquisition media cost.

That is not automatically net profit, and it does not establish available cash. It explains one part of the customer economics.

Check whether cash and inventory can support growth

A profitable sale may require payments before the customer’s cash is available. Inventory can require a deposit or full payment before it arrives. Advertising, fulfilment and operating commitments follow their own schedules.

Profit and cash therefore need separate, connected views.

A cash forecast places receipts and payments on dates. Its lowest projected balance can be more important than the closing balance at the end of a month.

In the shared example, forecast weekly closing cash reaches a low of $35,000 against a required reserve of $30,000. That leaves $5,000 of forecast headroom.

The figure depends on the forecast. It is not automatically $5,000 available for additional spending. A delayed receipt or earlier payment could reduce it.

Stock availability can limit an attractive opportunity

Inventory must also be read against expected demand and replenishment timing.

Example Commerce’s Everyday Kit has two weeks of stock cover, with confirmed replenishment arriving in four weeks. That creates a potential two-week gap under the stated demand assumption.

A marketing scenario cannot assume unlimited sales of that product while ignoring the expected shortage.

The opposite issue can also matter. Stock that sells slowly can retain cash the business needs elsewhere. A high stock balance does not establish excess on its own, but it identifies a position to examine.

Growth becomes supportable when the contribution, payment timing and stock requirements fit together.

Read the executive dashboard in a deliberate order

The dashboard is most useful when its sections form a connected assessment.

1. Establish the commercial result

Read net revenue, contribution after marketing and operating profit. Examine both dollars and margins.

Check whether the underlying costs are complete and whether any figures are provisional.

2. Compare the result

Examine the selected comparison and the surrounding trend. Check period coverage, seasonality and material changes in the business.

Do not rely on one period to explain a continuing pattern.

3. Examine acquisition and customer economics

Read media spending and efficiency alongside acquisition cost, its allowance, customer contribution and payback.

Keep blended period measures separate from cohort measures.

4. Check capacity to support growth

Examine the cash forecast, reserve, upcoming payments and important stock exceptions.

Identify whether the apparent opportunity depends on funding, replenishment or other unresolved conditions.

5. Select the question that needs investigation

The next step should follow the evidence.

A revenue increase with little additional contribution may require examination of discounts, product mix or fulfilment costs. Strong customer value with slow payback may require closer attention to funding. A stock gap may constrain a channel that is otherwise performing well.

These are different problems. They should not produce the same automatic recommendation.

Separate the observation from the explanation

A dashboard can show that a metric changed. A calculation can explain how that change affects the financial result. Establishing why it happened requires further evidence.

For example:

  • Revenue increased is an observation.
  • Additional contribution exceeded additional costs is a financial calculation.
  • A particular campaign caused the profit increase is a causal claim.

The third statement requires stronger evidence than the first two.

This distinction supports useful executive judgement. It allows the business to act on what is known while making uncertainty explicit.

It also prevents a favourable metric from concealing a constraint elsewhere. Strong advertising returns do not remove a stock shortage. Positive customer contribution does not remove a near-term cash obligation. More revenue does not guarantee more profit.
