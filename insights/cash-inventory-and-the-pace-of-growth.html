Cash, inventory and the pace of growth

By Collective

A business can increase sales and report a profit while its cash balance falls. It may need to pay for stock, advertising and operating costs before the resulting sales provide enough cash to recover that investment.

Profit measures the financial result under the stated accounting basis. Cash shows whether the business can meet payments when they fall due.

Growth needs both. Attractive customer economics establish a potential opportunity. Cash and inventory determine whether the business can support it, at what pace and under which conditions.

Profit and cash follow different timing

The earlier articles followed Example Commerce, an illustrative ecommerce business. In October, it recorded $270,000 in net revenue and $40,500 in operating profit.

That does not establish a $40,500 increase in its bank balance.

Several timing differences can separate profit from cash:

  • Inventory may be paid for before it is sold.
  • Customer payments may take time to settle.
  • Supplier invoices may be paid after the associated goods are sold.
  • Advertising and operating payments may fall due before related receipts arrive.
  • Tax, financing and other commitments may affect cash without appearing in the same operating-profit calculation.

The profit report and cash forecast therefore answer different questions.

The profit report explains what the trading activity earned after its included costs. The cash forecast places expected receipts and payments on dates.

Both need consistent definitions, but they should not be forced into identical figures.

Inventory purchases are not the same as COGS

Cost of goods sold, or COGS, represents the cost of the products sold during the reporting period.

Inventory purchases add stock that may be sold during that period or later. The associated payment follows the supplier’s terms.

A business might pay for a large shipment in November and sell the goods over several subsequent months. The cash leaves according to the payment schedule. The product cost enters the profit calculation as the goods are sold.

This distinction matters when assessing growth. More demand may require a larger purchase before the business receives the resulting sales proceeds.

It also matters when building a cash forecast. If inventory payments are already included, accounting COGS must not be deducted again as though it were another cash payment.

The same discipline applies elsewhere. Record each cash movement once, on its expected payment or receipt date.

Read the lowest forecast balance

A period’s ending cash balance can conceal a shortfall earlier in the period.

The executive needs to see the expected path of cash, including its lowest point and the commitments that create it.

Example Commerce begins November with $95,000 in cash. Its illustrative forecast includes customer receipts, inventory payments and other cash payments.

The cash amounts below include any applicable GST. Other payments include all remaining forecast cash obligations, including tax payments due. These amounts are separate from the GST-exclusive revenue and expenses in the profit examples.

The calculation for each week is:

Closing cash = opening cash + receipts − inventory payments − other payments.

The forecast closes on 28 November at $48,000, but the lowest projected weekly closing balance is $35,000 on 21 November.

That lower figure is important. Looking only at the forecast's final balance would miss the tighter position earlier in the period.

A weekly forecast can also conceal a lower balance within a week. Where payment timing is tight, the forecast needs sufficient detail to show the sequence of receipts and commitments.

Define the reserve before interpreting headroom

A required cash reserve expresses the minimum balance the business intends to preserve. Its size depends on the business’s obligations, uncertainty and operating requirements.

It should not be invented from a generic dashboard benchmark.

In this example, the business has set a $30,000 reserve.

MeasureAmount
Lowest forecast weekly closing cash$35,000
Required reserve$30,000
Forecast cash headroom$5,000

The $5,000 is headroom above the reserve under the stated forecast.

It is not automatically an amount available for additional advertising or another stock order. The forecast depends on assumptions about receipts, payment dates and costs. A change in any of those assumptions can reduce the headroom.

For example, if $10,000 of receipts expected by 21 November arrived the following week, the projected balance would fall to $25,000, all else unchanged. That would place it $5,000 below the reserve.

This is a timing scenario, not a prediction. It shows why the forecast should be read with its assumptions and commitments.

Stock cover connects inventory with demand

A stock balance is difficult to interpret without the rate at which the product is expected to sell.

Weeks of stock cover = available units ÷ expected weekly unit demand.

Example Commerce holds 240 units of Everyday Kit and expects demand of 120 units per week.

240 ÷ 120 = two weeks of stock cover.

The calculation uses units in both the stock balance and demand rate. Dividing inventory value in dollars by weekly unit sales would not produce a valid measure of cover.

The demand assumption also needs examination. Recent sales may understate demand if the product was unavailable. A promotion may increase future demand. Seasonality may make the most recent weekly average unsuitable.

Stock cover is therefore a forward-looking estimate when it uses expected demand. It is not a guarantee of the date on which stock will run out.

Compare cover with replenishment timing

Stock cover becomes more useful when compared with the time until additional stock becomes available for sale.

For Everyday Kit:

MeasurePosition at 31 October
Available stock240 units
Expected weekly demand120 units
Current stock coverTwo weeks
Full replenishment lead timeFive weeks
Confirmed incoming stock600 units
Time until incoming stock arrivesFour weeks

At the assumed demand rate, current stock lasts two weeks. Confirmed replenishment arrives in four weeks. That creates a potential two-week gap.

The full five-week lead time and the four-week remaining arrival time describe different things. The existing order is already in progress.

A new order placed now would follow the full applicable replenishment process. That may include production, transport, receiving and any preparation required before the goods can be sold.

The stock-gap calculation does not establish the exact sales or profit that would be lost. Customers might wait, buy another product or purchase elsewhere. The gap identifies an operating constraint that needs investigation.

It also changes the interpretation of a marketing opportunity. A profitable acquisition scenario cannot assume unlimited product availability when the product is expected to run out.

Examine important products individually

A healthy total inventory value can conceal shortages in the products driving demand.

It can also conceal cash tied up in products selling slowly.

Alongside Everyday Kit, Example Commerce holds:

Refill Pack’s incoming stock is expected before the current supply runs out, assuming the demand forecast holds.

Accessory Set has substantially more cover. That is a reason to examine the stock position, not sufficient evidence that it is excessive.

The appropriate holding may depend on seasonality, supplier reliability, order quantities, product availability and the consequences of running out. The value of the stock and its contribution also matter.

The executive view should highlight material exceptions rather than hide them within one blended stock-cover figure.

Growth can create stepped cash commitments

Sales may increase gradually while purchasing commitments increase in larger increments.

A supplier may require a minimum order quantity or a deposit. Freight costs may change at shipment thresholds. Supporting a higher sales rate may require another employee, more storage or additional fulfilment capacity.

These commitments can create a step in the cash requirement.

The business therefore needs to assess more than the expected contribution from the next order. It must consider the payments required to make that order, and the orders after it, possible.

A profitable growth scenario may require more cash than the current balance can support. Conversely, a more gradual increase may allow receipts and contribution to accumulate before the next commitment.

The appropriate pace depends on the business’s objectives and constraints. Slower growth is not automatically better, and faster growth is not automatically sustainable.

The forecast makes the trade-off visible.

Connect acquisition payback with inventory funding

The previous article’s illustrative June cohort recovered its allocated acquisition media cost through cumulative contribution by day 45.

That describes contribution payback. It does not mean every associated cash outlay was funded or recovered on the same schedule.

The business may have paid for the products before acquiring those customers. It may need to place another stock order before the cohort reaches payback. Some customer receipts may settle after the sale.

A complete growth assessment therefore connects:

  • Acquisition spending.
  • Contribution from first and subsequent purchases.
  • Customer receipt timing.
  • Inventory deposits and payments.
  • Fulfilment and operating commitments.
  • The required reserve.

Strong eventual customer value does not remove the need to fund the period before recovery.

It also does not establish that repeating the same acquisition volume is affordable. Overlapping cohorts can increase the total amount awaiting recovery while inventory requirements grow.

Test growth against the dated cash forecast

A commercial scenario should show both its expected profit effect and its cash consequences.

Begin with the existing forecast. Add the proposed activity’s receipts and payments at the times they are expected to occur.

Include the associated stock requirement. Do not assume that additional sales can be fulfilled from stock already committed elsewhere.

Then examine:

  1. The additional operating contribution.
  2. The lowest projected cash balance.
  3. The timing and size of any reserve shortfall.
  4. Product availability during the proposed activity.
  5. The assumptions most capable of changing the result.

An adverse case might include slower sales, delayed settlement, higher fulfilment costs or a later stock arrival. The purpose is to understand which conditions the scenario depends on.

If funding is required, show the requirement explicitly. Do not treat hypothetical financing or an unconfirmed supplier extension as available cash.

What to examine on the dashboard

Read cash headroom alongside the forecast balance, reserve and low-point date. Then inspect the payments and receipts around that date.

A small positive headroom figure may still leave little tolerance for timing changes. A comfortable month-end balance may conceal an earlier shortfall.

For inventory, examine the products that materially affect sales and contribution. Compare stock cover with confirmed replenishment, using a demand assumption appropriate to the coming period.

Ask:

  • Are customer receipts likely to arrive when forecast?
  • Which payments are committed, and which are estimates?
  • Does the forecast include all material obligations?
  • Could important products run out before replenishment?
  • Does proposed growth require a larger or earlier stock purchase?
  • Would a weaker sales response or timing delay breach the reserve?

These questions connect the commercial opportunity with the business’s ability to support it.

Revenue, contribution and profit explain the result. Customer economics explains acquisition affordability and recovery. Cash and inventory establish the timing constraints within which that model must operate.
