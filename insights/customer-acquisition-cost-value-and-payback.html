Customer acquisition cost, customer value and payback

By David Collins

Acquiring a customer has a cost. Whether that cost is affordable depends on what the customer contributes, when that contribution arrives and what the business must fund in between.

A first purchase may cover acquisition immediately. Another customer may require several purchases before their contribution recovers the acquisition cost.

Customer acquisition cost, customer value and payback describe different parts of that relationship. Read together, they help assess the economics of acquiring customers without mistaking revenue for profit or future value for available cash.

Define the customer being acquired

Customer acquisition cost, or CAC, relates acquisition expenditure to new customers. The numerator and denominator need to describe the same scope.

A new customer is someone making their first purchase from the business, based on the available customer history. This differs from an order, a website session or a platform conversion.

A returning customer can place several orders. An advertising platform can report purchases from both new and returning customers. Dividing expenditure by those purchases produces a cost per purchase, not necessarily a cost per new customer.

The distinction matters because first purchases and repeat purchases can have different economics. A blended order count does not reveal how many customers the business acquired.

Customer identity also needs consistent treatment. Incomplete purchase history or duplicate customer records can make an existing customer appear new.

Before interpreting CAC, establish who is counted and which costs are included.

Separate business media CAC from cohort acquisition cost

A useful business-level measure is:

Business media CAC = period paid media spend ÷ first-time customers purchasing in that period.

Example Commerce, the illustrative business used throughout this series, records the following in October:

MeasureOctober 2026
Paid media spend$45,000
First-time customers900
Business media CAC$50

This shows that the business spent $50 on paid media for each first-time customer recorded during the month.

It does not establish that every customer was acquired by that spending. Some may have arrived through unpaid channels. Some expenditure may have supported returning customers or purchases in another period.

Business media CAC is therefore a blended period measure. It is useful for monitoring the relationship between investment and customer acquisition, but it is not a precise cost allocation to each customer.

Cohort acquisition CAC uses acquisition costs allocated to a defined group of customers, such as those first acquired in June.

That allocation must be explained. It should not be presented as an exact customer-level fact if it relies on assumptions.

A fully loaded acquisition measure may also include agency fees, creative production and other acquisition costs. It answers a broader cost question than media CAC. Its allowance and profitability calculation must use the same cost scope.

Distinguish actual CAC from allowable CAC

Actual CAC describes a measured or allocated cost. Allowable CAC describes what the business can afford under specified economic requirements.

The allowance begins with the contribution available from the relevant customers.

For a first-order assessment, use first-order revenue and costs. An average across all orders may include returning customers with different basket sizes, product choices and purchasing behaviour.

Example Commerce’s illustrative October first-order economics are:

MeasurePer first order
Net revenue$160
Product costs$72
Fulfilment and transaction costs$16
Contribution before acquisition media$72

The first order contributes $72 before acquisition media, other marketing costs, overhead and profit requirements.

Spending the entire $72 on acquisition media would leave nothing from that order for those remaining requirements.

Suppose the business allows $12 for overhead and other marketing costs and requires $5 in first-order profit.

CalculationAmount
Contribution before acquisition media$72
Overhead and other marketing allowance−$12
Desired first-order profit−$5
Allowable first-order media CAC$55

The $55 is a media allowance under those assumptions. It is not a universal acquisition target.

If product margin, fulfilment costs or the required profit changes, the allowance changes. If the calculation becomes negative, the proposed first-order requirement is not achievable under those economics.

The October business media CAC of $50 sits below this illustrative $55 allowance. That is a useful initial comparison, not proof that every acquired customer meets the requirement. The blended measure still needs the population and allocation checks described earlier.

Higher order value helps only when contribution increases

A larger first order can support a higher acquisition allowance, but only if it leaves more contribution after the associated costs.

Discounted bundles, low-margin accessories and heavier shipping requirements can increase order value without increasing contribution by the same proportion.

The relevant question is therefore not simply whether average order value increased. It is whether contribution per acquired customer increased, and what happened to purchase volume and customer quality.

A post-purchase offer may also add contribution after the initial sale. Its costs, acceptance rate and refunds need to be included before treating it as additional acquisition capacity.

The revenue measure identifies the sale. The contribution calculation establishes what remains available.

Define customer value by its financial basis and time horizon

Lifetime value, commonly shortened to LTV, can refer to several different measures.

MeasureWhat it describes
Revenue LTVRevenue generated per acquired customer over the stated horizon
Gross-profit LTVThat revenue less the corresponding product costs
Contribution LTVThe amount remaining after the additional costs included in the contribution definition

These measures are not interchangeable.

Revenue LTV shows purchasing value. Contribution LTV is more directly relevant when assessing how much remains to recover acquisition cost.

For the examples in this article, contribution LTV deducts product, fulfilment, transaction and applicable retention costs. It is measured before the initial acquisition media cost, which is then compared or deducted separately.

The time horizon must remain visible. A 90-day contribution figure and a 12-month revenue figure answer different questions.

An observed twelve-month value also requires twelve months of customer history. A younger cohort may have a forecast twelve-month value, but that forecast should remain separate from what has already occurred.

Use cohorts to understand how value develops

A cohort is a defined group of customers whose performance is followed over time. Grouping customers by the month of their first purchase helps compare how contribution develops after acquisition.

For an acquisition-month cohort, cumulative contribution per customer is:

Total contribution generated by the cohort to the stated age ÷ the original number of acquired customers.

The denominator includes customers who do not purchase again. Removing them would overstate the value of an average acquired customer.

Compare cohorts at equivalent ages. A cohort with twelve months of purchasing history has had more opportunity to contribute than one acquired three months ago.

Product mix, promotions, acquisition sources and customer behaviour may also differ. Cohort analysis makes those differences available for investigation; it does not automatically explain them.

A period’s returning-customer share is a different measure. It describes the customer mix purchasing during that period, not the proportion of an original acquisition cohort that has returned.

Payback measures the time required to recover acquisition cost

Acquisition payback is the time required for cumulative customer contribution to recover the specified acquisition cost.

Consider a separate illustrative cohort first acquired in June. Its economics are distinct from the October first-order example.

Cohort measureValue
Customers acquired680
Acquisition media allocated to the cohort$28,560
Acquisition media CAC$42

The cohort’s cumulative contribution develops as follows:

Cohort ageContribution per acquired customer before initial acquisition media
First purchase$30
Day 30$38
Day 45$42
Day 60$49
Day 90$61

The initial $30 contribution does not cover the $42 acquisition media cost. It leaves $12 per customer unrecovered.

Cumulative contribution reaches $42 at day 45. Under this illustrative record, acquisition media payback occurs at that point and remains recovered thereafter.

At day 90:

$61 contribution − $42 acquisition media cost = $19 contribution per acquired customer after initial acquisition media.

That $19 is not automatically net profit. Any costs outside the stated contribution and acquisition definitions still need to be accounted for.

The example shows why a first-order shortfall and an eventual positive contribution can coexist. It also shows why the time between them matters.

Count acquisition and retention costs once

Payback calculations can become misleading when costs are omitted or counted twice.

The June example deducts applicable retention costs when calculating customer contribution. It compares that contribution with the initial acquisition media allocation.

It would be incorrect to include the same retention expenditure again in the acquisition cost being recovered.

This is a particular risk when a blended media CAC includes both acquisition and retention spending, while the customer-value calculation also deducts retention costs.

Keep the cost allocation consistent:

  • Initial acquisition costs belong in the acquisition amount being assessed.
  • Subsequent retention costs reduce the contribution available from later purchases.
  • Other allocated costs must have an explicit position.
  • No expense should be deducted twice from the same result.

Where the allocation cannot be supported, disclose the limitation. A precise-looking payback figure is not a substitute for a defensible cost basis.

Read the timing as well as the eventual value

Two cohorts can produce the same contribution after twelve months while recovering acquisition cost at very different times.

The faster-repaying cohort generally leaves less acquisition investment unrecovered during that period. The business still needs to examine when receipts arrive and costs are paid.

Contribution payback and cash payback are related, but they are not identical. Inventory may have been paid for before the first purchase. Customer receipts may settle later. Refunds or chargebacks may reverse contribution that initially appeared recovered.

An observed payback assessment should therefore state the maturity of the data and whether recovery remains intact through the observed period.

If the cohort has not yet recovered its acquisition cost, describe it as not yet paid back within the observed window. Do not assign a future payback date without identifying it as a forecast.

A ratio does not replace the underlying economics

LTV divided by CAC summarises customer value relative to acquisition cost. Its interpretation depends on which LTV and CAC are used.

A revenue-based ratio does not show how much remains after product and fulfilment costs. A contribution-based ratio provides a different reading. A ratio calculated from forecast value carries the uncertainty of that forecast.

The horizon also matters. The same eventual ratio can involve very different repayment times.

There is no single ratio that establishes affordable acquisition for every business. The assessment must account for margin, repeat behaviour, cost scope, timing and available funding.

The useful question is whether the acquired customers produce sufficient contribution, within a period the business can support.

What to examine on the dashboard

Begin with new-customer cost and its definition. Establish whether it is a blended period measure, an allocated cohort cost or a fully loaded acquisition measure.

Then examine the corresponding allowance and customer contribution:

  • Does the first-order calculation use the relevant new-customer population?
  • Which costs are included in the allowance?
  • Is customer value expressed as revenue or contribution?
  • Is the value observed or forecast?
  • Are cohorts being compared at the same age?
  • Are acquisition and retention costs counted once?
  • Has acquisition cost been recovered within the observed period?

Keep the period and cohort views separate. October’s blended media CAC should not be paired with June’s cohort contribution as though they describe the same customers.

Once the customer economics are understood, the remaining question is whether the business can fund the timing. A positive contribution after several months does not pay a supplier invoice due today.

That connection between profit, inventory and available cash determines the pace at which an otherwise viable acquisition model can grow.
