Revenue, contribution and operating profit

By Collective

Revenue shows what a business sells. Contribution shows what remains after specified costs. Operating profit shows what remains after the operating costs included in the accounts.

Reading these figures together explains how sales translate into a commercial result. It also helps identify why revenue can grow without producing the profit an owner expects.

The first step is to understand what each figure includes. Two reports can use the same label for different calculations. A useful dashboard makes those definitions consistent and accessible.

Start with the revenue figure

For the examples in this article, net revenue means sales after discounts and refunds, excluding GST. All figures are illustrative and expressed in Australian dollars.

This basis matters. Discounts reduce the amount earned from a sale. Refunds reverse some or all of it. GST collected is not revenue available to cover costs or retain as profit.

Shipping income also needs consistent treatment. A business may include it in its reported revenue, but that income should remain distinguishable from product sales. The example below assumes no shipping income.

The same discipline applies to returns. A returned product, its refund and any associated handling costs are related, but they are not three equivalent deductions from revenue. Record the revenue reversal once, then account for the stock and processing effects separately.

A dashboard’s revenue figure should therefore answer a precise question: what revenue has the business retained from the sales included in this period?

Gross profit shows the product economics

Gross profit is net revenue less the cost of goods sold, or COGS.

COGS represents the cost of the products sold during the period. Depending on the business’s accounting policy, this may include manufacturing or purchase costs, inbound freight, duties and other costs of bringing those goods into stock.

It is different from the amount paid to purchase inventory during the month. Some purchased stock may remain unsold. Some products sold this month may have been purchased and paid for earlier.

Consider an illustrative ecommerce business, Example Commerce. In October, it records:

MeasureOctober
Net revenue$270,000
Cost of goods sold$121,500
Gross profit$148,500
Gross margin55.0%

Gross margin expresses gross profit as a percentage of revenue:

Gross margin = gross profit ÷ net revenue

The business retains 55 cents from each revenue dollar after its product costs. That money must still cover fulfilment, transaction fees, marketing and the remaining operating expenses.

Gross margin helps explain product economics, but it does not establish the final profitability of an order or the business.

Contribution shows what remains after specified costs

Selling a product creates costs beyond the product itself. These may include outbound shipping, pick-and-pack charges, payment processing and other variable costs associated with the sale.

For Example Commerce, fulfilment and transaction costs total $27,000 in October.

MeasureOctober
Gross profit$148,500
Fulfilment and transaction costs$27,000
Contribution before marketing$121,500

Here, contribution before marketing means revenue remaining after product, fulfilment and transaction costs.

It represents 45% of net revenue. That is the amount available to cover marketing, the remaining operating costs and profit.

The qualifier matters. “Contribution” is used differently across businesses and reporting tools. Some calculations deduct advertising. Others do not. Some include fulfilment costs within COGS.

A dashboard should state which costs have been deducted. Each cost must appear once, regardless of the category used.

Deduct marketing costs

Example Commerce spends $45,000 on paid media and $9,000 on other marketing costs during October.

Other marketing costs in this example include agency, creative and lifecycle marketing costs.

MeasureOctober
Contribution before marketing$121,500
Paid media$45,000
Other marketing costs$9,000
Contribution after marketing$67,500
Contribution after marketing margin25.0%

The business has $67,500 remaining to cover its other operating expenses and produce operating profit.

This reporting subtotal can include fixed marketing costs. Its margin is therefore not automatically the variable contribution rate available from additional sales.

Contribution after marketing is useful because it connects the cost of generating and serving sales with the amount those sales leave behind. It prevents revenue or advertising returns from being read without their associated costs.

It is still not the final operating result.

Operating profit accounts for the remaining expenses

A business also incurs costs such as wages, rent, software, insurance and administration.

Some costs may already be included in the earlier calculations. Only the remaining expenses should be deducted at this stage. An agency fee included in marketing costs, for example, must not be deducted again as overhead.

Example Commerce has $27,000 of remaining operating expenses.

MeasureOctober
Contribution after marketing$67,500
Remaining operating expenses$27,000
Operating profit$40,500
Operating margin15.0%

The complete calculation is:

$270,000 revenue − $121,500 COGS − $27,000 fulfilment and transaction costs − $54,000 marketing costs − $27,000 remaining operating expenses = $40,500 operating profit.

Operating margin expresses that result as a percentage of revenue:

Operating margin = operating profit ÷ net revenue

Example Commerce retains 15 cents in operating profit from each revenue dollar under this cost model.

Operating profit is not necessarily net profit

Net profit requires the additional items applicable to the business, including tax and any non-operating income or expenses.

A dashboard that includes sales, product costs and advertising, but omits other material expenses, cannot establish net profit. It should name the subtotal it can support.

Missing costs also need to remain visible. An unavailable expense is not a zero expense. If a material cost is still estimated or awaiting confirmation, the resulting profit figure should carry that qualification.

Read profit dollars and profit margin together

Profit dollars show the absolute result. Profit margin shows how much of each revenue dollar remains.

Both matter because they can move in different directions.

Example Commerce’s September and October results illustrate this:

MeasureSeptemberOctober
Net revenue$240,000$270,000
Gross margin55.0%55.0%
Contribution before marketing$108,000$121,500
Paid media$36,000$45,000
Other marketing costs$8,000$9,000
Contribution after marketing$64,000$67,500
Remaining operating expenses$26,000$27,000
Operating profit$38,000$40,500
Operating margin15.83%15.00%

Revenue increased by $30,000. Operating profit increased by $2,500. Operating margin fell by approximately 0.83 percentage points.

The business made more operating profit while retaining a smaller proportion of its revenue.

Neither figure should be read alone. Looking only at margin would miss the increase in profit dollars. Looking only at profit dollars would miss the increased cost of producing that result.

Whether the trade-off is worthwhile depends on the business’s objectives, the durability of the result and its cash requirements.

Explain the change through its components

The difference between the two periods can be reconciled directly:

ChangeEffect on operating profit
Additional contribution before marketing+$13,500
Additional paid media−$9,000
Additional other marketing costs−$1,000
Additional operating expenses−$1,000
Change in operating profit+$2,500

This explains the arithmetic of the profit increase.

It does not establish that the additional advertising caused the additional revenue. Product demand, returning customers, promotions and other factors may also have changed. Establishing the cause requires further evidence.

The financial calculation tells us what changed in the result. Diagnosis examines why.

More revenue does not always mean more contribution

An increase in sales can carry a different cost structure.

Discounting may generate more orders while reducing the contribution from each order. A larger basket may include lower-margin products or incur higher delivery costs. A change in product mix may increase revenue while reducing the proportion remaining after COGS.

Average order value is therefore useful, but it should be read alongside contribution per order and total contribution.

The same principle applies to sales volume. More orders with less contribution per order may increase total contribution, leave it unchanged or reduce it. The result depends on the size of both changes.

This is why a dashboard should connect sales measures with their economic consequences. Revenue describes the sales result. Contribution shows what those sales leave available for the remaining costs and profit.

What to examine on the dashboard

Begin with net revenue, contribution after marketing and operating profit. Read their dollar values, margins and changes over the same period.

If revenue rises but operating profit does not, examine the deductions between them:

  • Gross margin: Did product costs, pricing, discounts or product mix change?
  • Fulfilment and transaction costs: Did the cost of serving each order increase?
  • Marketing costs: Did media or other marketing expenditure grow faster than contribution before marketing?
  • Remaining operating expenses: Did additional capacity or overhead absorb the contribution increase?

These are investigation questions, not conclusions. The relevant comparison period and the quality of the underlying data still matter.

Also check whether the dashboard includes all material costs and whether any values are provisional. A precise-looking profit figure can still be incomplete.

Finally, keep profit separate from cash. Example Commerce’s $40,500 operating profit does not mean its bank balance increased by $40,500. Inventory payments, settlement timing and other commitments can produce a different cash result.

A useful executive view keeps those readings connected while preserving what each one means.
