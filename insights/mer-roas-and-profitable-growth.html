MER, ROAS and profitable growth

By Collective

Marketing efficiency describes the relationship between spending and revenue. It does not, by itself, establish whether the business should spend more or less.

A higher return can accompany limited sales volume. A lower return can accompany greater absolute profit. Additional spending can also reduce profit if the resulting sales do not cover their associated costs.

The executive question is therefore specific: what would additional marketing investment contribute after the additional costs, and can the business support it?

MER and ROAS help inform that question. They measure different things and need to be read alongside the business’s contribution, profit and constraints.

MER measures business revenue against media spend

Marketing efficiency ratio, or MER, is business revenue divided by paid media spend.

In this series, the revenue figure is net revenue after discounts and refunds, excluding GST. The denominator includes paid media. Other marketing costs remain separate and are included when calculating profit.

For Example Commerce, the illustrative business used throughout these articles:

MeasureOctober 2026
Net revenue$270,000
Paid media spend$45,000
MER6.00

The business records six dollars of net revenue for each dollar of paid media spend.

That does not mean paid media caused all six dollars. Business revenue also reflects returning customers, unpaid discovery, email activity, existing demand and other influences.

MER is useful because it relates total business revenue to total media spending without adding competing platform revenue claims. It is a business-level efficiency measure, not a calculation of advertising’s causal effect.

Some reports use total marketing costs rather than paid media in the denominator. That produces a different measure. The cost basis should remain explicit whenever ratios are compared.

ROAS measures attributed revenue against advertising spend

Return on ad spend, or ROAS, is attributed conversion value divided by the corresponding advertising spend.

Its meaning depends on what the platform counts as a conversion, how it values that conversion and how it assigns credit.

Example Commerce’s illustrative October platform results are:

PlatformMedia spendAttributed purchase valueROAS
Google Ads$27,000$162,0006.00
Meta Ads$18,000$126,0007.00

These figures describe what each platform reports under its attribution settings. They do not establish that Google and Meta independently generated those amounts of additional business revenue.

A customer may discover a product through Meta, later search through Google and then purchase. Depending on the settings, both platforms may receive credit.

The two attributed values total $288,000, while the business records $270,000 in net revenue. That difference requires interpretation. Attribution overlap, revenue definitions and reporting dates may all contribute.

Do not add platform-attributed revenue and call the result business revenue. Equally, do not assume every discrepancy proves a tracking defect.

ROAS remains useful for examining performance within a platform, provided the conversion definitions and reporting conditions are understood.

Read efficiency alongside contribution and profit

Example Commerce’s September and October results show why MER should not be treated as the final objective.

All figures are illustrative, in Australian dollars and excluding GST.

MeasureSeptemberOctober
Net revenue$240,000$270,000
Paid media spend$36,000$45,000
MER6.676.00
Contribution after marketing$64,000$67,500
Operating profit$38,000$40,500
Operating margin15.83%15.00%

MER declined because media spending grew faster than revenue. Operating profit nevertheless increased by $2,500.

The additional revenue produced $13,500 of contribution before marketing. Additional media, other marketing and operating costs totalled $11,000. The difference increased operating profit.

This explains how the financial result changed. It does not prove that additional media caused the revenue increase.

The executive reading should retain both observations:

  • The business produced more operating profit.
  • It retained a smaller proportion of revenue and recorded a lower MER.

Whether that trade remains attractive depends on the durability of the result, the response to further spending and the business’s cash and stock position.

Historical returns do not establish the return on additional spending

Average performance describes the activity already undertaken. Marginal performance describes the result associated with an additional amount of activity.

The distinction matters when considering a budget increase.

A campaign’s existing ROAS may reflect a combination of branded demand, returning customers, high-intent searches and other audiences. Additional spending may reach a different mix.

It may also change auction costs, product mix or the proportion of customers who would have purchased through another route.

A historical ROAS of 7.00 therefore does not establish that the next dollar will produce seven dollars of additional business revenue.

The same limitation applies to MER. A strong business ratio may indicate effective marketing, substantial unpaid demand, conservative spending or several influences together. It does not identify how much profitable opportunity remains.

To examine additional spending, state the expected response as an assumption and test its commercial consequences.

Calculate the contribution from additional sales

The relevant calculation is:

Additional operating profit = additional revenue − associated product and variable costs − additional marketing costs − additional operating costs.

A contribution rate can simplify this calculation, provided its cost inclusions are clear.

For the following scenarios, assume:

  • Additional media spend is $5,000.
  • Additional sales retain 45% of revenue after product, fulfilment and transaction costs.
  • Further marketing and operating costs total $1,000.
  • Those additional costs are not already deducted in the 45% contribution rate.

These are prospective scenarios, separate from Example Commerce’s observed monthly comparison. The assumed sales response is not a verified forecast.

MeasureStronger responseWeaker response
Additional media spend$5,000$5,000
Assumed additional net revenue$20,000$12,000
Contribution rate before additional marketing45%45%
Additional contribution$9,000$5,400
Further marketing and operating costs$1,000$1,000
Additional operating profit$3,000−$600

In the stronger response, the additional contribution covers the $5,000 media investment and $1,000 of further costs, leaving $3,000.

In the weaker response, the same costs exceed the additional contribution by $600.

Both scenarios increase revenue. Only one increases operating profit.

Read the proposed total as well as the increment

Applying the stronger scenario to October would produce:

  • Net revenue of $290,000.
  • Paid media spend of $50,000.
  • Operating profit of $43,500.
  • MER of 5.80.

MER would fall from 6.00 to 5.80 while operating profit increased by $3,000.

This is a calculated consequence of the assumptions. It is not evidence that the business can achieve the assumed response.

The scenario tells us what would need to be true for the additional investment to be worthwhile.

Derive the break-even requirement from the costs

In these scenarios, additional contribution must cover $6,000: the $5,000 media investment plus $1,000 in further costs.

At a 45% contribution rate, the required additional revenue is:

$6,000 ÷ 45% = approximately $13,333.

Relative to the additional $5,000 of media, that is approximately 2.67 dollars of additional business revenue per media dollar.

This is a scenario-specific break-even requirement. It is not a universal ROAS target or an instruction to enter 2.67 into an advertising platform.

The distinction is important. The calculation uses additional business revenue on a defined net basis. A platform target may use attributed conversion value with different tax, shipping, customer or attribution treatment.

The threshold also changes if the additional sales have a different margin or require different costs.

A discounted product mix may reduce contribution. Higher fulfilment costs may do the same. An additional agency fee or staffing requirement may increase the amount the investment must recover.

The economics must describe the proposed activity, not simply repeat an average from the existing business.

Allow for changing returns as spending increases

One scenario cannot establish an optimal budget.

Applying the same assumed return to every spending level creates a linear forecast. It cannot reveal whether additional auctions, audiences or products become less attractive as spending expands.

A useful assessment examines several spending levels and their associated assumptions:

  • Expected additional sales.
  • Product and customer mix.
  • Acquisition and fulfilment costs.
  • Further marketing or operating commitments.
  • Stock availability.
  • The timing of cash payments and receipts.

The assumptions may change at each level. A modest increase may fit existing capacity, while a larger increase requires more stock, production or staff.

A business may also have more profitable uses for the same money. Improving conversion, increasing order contribution or retaining customers may deserve examination alongside additional media.

The purpose is to compare credible opportunities, not to assume that spending more is always the next step.

Keep attribution, calculation and causality separate

Three statements can look similar while requiring different evidence.

“The platform reports a ROAS of 6.00.”
This is an attributed platform observation.

“At the assumed sales response and costs, this scenario adds $3,000 operating profit.”
This is a calculation.

“The additional advertising produced $3,000 more operating profit than the business would otherwise have earned.”
This is a causal claim.

The third statement requires evidence about what would have happened without the additional advertising. A simple before-and-after comparison may be affected by seasonality, promotions, stock, pricing or changes elsewhere in the business.

Where the evidence supports only an association or a scenario, describe it that way. Precision in the arithmetic does not remove uncertainty in the assumptions.

What to examine on the dashboard

Read marketing return and media spend alongside contribution after marketing and operating profit.

If MER falls, examine whether profit dollars also fell, which costs changed and whether the comparison period is appropriate.

If ROAS rises, examine the revenue basis, customer and campaign mix, and the corresponding business result. An improved platform ratio does not automatically establish improved business economics.

Before interpreting high efficiency as permission to spend more, identify:

  • The expected response to additional spending.
  • The contribution of the additional sales.
  • Any further costs required.
  • The strength of the supporting evidence.
  • Stock and cash constraints.

Customer economics adds another part of this assessment. Some acquired customers contribute beyond their first order. Others do not recover their acquisition cost within a period the business can fund.

Understanding that difference requires examining acquisition cost, customer value and payback together.
