When revenue grows and profit doesn’t

By Collective

More revenue creates an opportunity. What the business keeps depends on the work and cost required to earn it.

In this note

A growing sales report deserves attention. It also deserves a second question: what changed underneath that number? A business can sell more while spending more to acquire customers, discounting more heavily or taking on work that costs more to deliver.

The useful response starts with the economics of the additional sales. It asks which customers, products and activities created them, then follows the costs far enough to understand their contribution.

Agree what you are measuring

Start with one period and a consistent definition of revenue. Allow for discounts, cancellations and returns where they apply. Then name the costs included in the comparison. Product cost, fulfilment, payment fees, acquisition, delivery and service support can all change the result.

Keep the same boundary on both sides. If one period includes marketing management and the other includes only advertising spend, the comparison cannot tell you whether growth became more worthwhile. Count each cost once and record anything excluded.

Follow the additional sale

A blended average can hide a changing customer mix. Separate new and returning customers where that distinction helps. Look at product groups, offers or service types with materially different margins and delivery requirements.

Then examine what it took to produce the increase. Was it a stronger offer, more advertising, repeat purchase, a promotion or a change in price? Timing alone does not establish cause, but it gives the investigation somewhere specific to begin.

Illustrative example. Suppose additional revenue is $40,000 and the contribution margin is 40% after the defined variable delivery costs, before acquisition and service investment. That leaves $16,000 at this boundary. If the additional acquisition and service investment totals $18,000, the activity is $2,000 behind at that boundary.

This is an example of the calculation, not a client result or a forecast. It assumes the costs are complete, consistent and not counted twice. It does not describe total business profit.

Choose the decision the numbers support

The next move depends on what the review finds. A discount may have attracted customers whose orders leave little contribution. A popular product may create unusually high delivery costs. An acquisition campaign may work well for one customer group and poorly for another.

Each suggests a different action: change the offer, adjust the product mix, examine delivery or refine acquisition. The question is which intervention addresses the observed constraint and how the team will know whether it helped.

Keep time and capacity in view

A sale and a cash receipt can happen at different times. Stock, onboarding and delivery may need funding before the customer pays. A profitable engagement can also absorb capacity needed elsewhere.

Review those requirements alongside the contribution calculation. State when costs fall due, when revenue is collected and what the business must deliver. If repeat purchase is part of the case, use observed customer behaviour and a stated review period rather than assuming future value has already been earned.

Give growth a useful next step

A commercial review should end with a decision, an owner and a point to revisit the evidence. Keep the result visible alongside the revenue number. That gives the business a clearer way to decide what to continue, what to change and where to invest next.

That is the question behind grow what you keep.
