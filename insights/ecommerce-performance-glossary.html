Ecommerce performance glossary

By Collective

This glossary defines the commercial measures used in the executive dashboard and accompanying articles.

In this note
Revenue and ordersCosts, contribution and profitMarketing efficiency and additional spendingCustomers, acquisition and valueCash and inventoryComparisons and interpretation

A metric’s meaning depends on its revenue basis, included costs, customer population and reporting period. Keep those definitions consistent when comparing results.

The examples in this series use Australian dollars. Revenue and profit examples exclude GST. Cash forecasts use illustrative receipts and payments, including applicable tax movements.

The dashboard label Marketing return refers to MER. New customer cost refers to business media CAC.

Revenue and orders

Net revenue

Revenue remaining after the specified reductions, such as discounts and refunds, on the stated tax basis.

In this series, net revenue excludes GST. Shipping income is zero in the illustrative business.

Calculation: Sales revenue − discounts − revenue reversals, with any other included trading income identified separately.

Count each monetary reversal once. A returned product and its refund should not automatically become two deductions from revenue.

Order

A purchase record included under the business’s reporting policy.

Order counts should state how cancelled, test, refunded and other excluded orders are treated. An order is not the same as a customer, website session or platform conversion.

Average order value

The average revenue associated with the orders in a defined population. Often abbreviated to AOV.

Calculation: Revenue from the included orders ÷ number of included orders.

Use matching revenue and order populations. AOV across all orders may differ from first-order AOV.

First-order average value

The average revenue from customers’ first purchases.

Calculation: Revenue from included first orders ÷ number of included first orders.

This is the relevant starting point for first-order acquisition economics. Returning customers may have different purchasing behaviour and order values.

Revenue growth

The change in revenue against a stated comparison.

Absolute change: Current revenue − comparison revenue.

Relative change: (Current revenue ÷ comparison revenue) − 1.

Revenue growth does not establish profit growth. The costs associated with the additional sales must also be examined.

Costs, contribution and profit

Cost of goods sold

The cost assigned to products sold during the reporting period. Commonly abbreviated to COGS.

The cost basis may include product purchase or manufacturing costs, inbound freight, duties and other costs included under the business’s accounting policy.

Calculation: Cost of the goods sold, with applicable adjustments.

COGS differs from inventory purchases paid for during the period. Purchased goods may remain unsold.

Gross profit

Revenue remaining after COGS.

Calculation: Net revenue − COGS.

Gross profit must still cover the costs excluded from COGS, including applicable fulfilment, marketing and operating expenses.

Gross margin

Gross profit expressed as a percentage of net revenue.

Calculation: Gross profit ÷ net revenue × 100.

Do not confuse gross margin with COGS as a percentage of revenue. They describe the remaining amount and the deducted amount respectively.

Fulfilment and transaction costs

The included costs of serving and processing orders beyond the product costs already recorded in COGS.

These may include outbound shipping, pick-and-pack charges, payment processing and applicable packaging costs.

Count each expense once. If a cost is already included in COGS, do not deduct it again here.

Contribution before marketing

Revenue remaining after product costs and the included fulfilment, transaction and other variable costs, before marketing.

Calculation used in this series: Net revenue − COGS − fulfilment and transaction costs.

This amount is available to cover marketing, remaining operating expenses and profit.

Contribution rate before marketing

Contribution before marketing expressed as a percentage of net revenue.

Calculation: Contribution before marketing ÷ net revenue × 100.

A rate used in a growth scenario should reflect the expected economics of the additional sales. A historical average is not automatically suitable.

Paid media spend

Expenditure on advertising placements across the included paid platforms.

In this series, paid media excludes agency, creative and lifecycle service costs. Those sit within other marketing costs.

Keep that distinction when calculating MER or media CAC.

Other marketing costs

Marketing expenditure outside the paid media amount.

In the illustrative business, this includes agency, creative and lifecycle marketing costs.

These expenses are deducted when calculating contribution after marketing. They must not be deducted again within remaining operating expenses.

Contribution after marketing

The amount remaining after the product, fulfilment, transaction and marketing costs included in the calculation.

Calculation used in this series: Contribution before marketing − paid media spend − other marketing costs.

It remains available to cover the remaining operating expenses and produce operating profit.

Contribution after marketing margin

Contribution after marketing expressed as a percentage of net revenue.

Calculation: Contribution after marketing ÷ net revenue × 100.

Compare it only with figures using the same cost inclusions.

The subtotal can include fixed marketing costs. Its margin is not automatically the variable contribution rate for additional sales.

Remaining operating expenses

Operating costs not already deducted in the contribution calculation.

These may include wages, rent, software, insurance and administration, depending on the business’s cost classification.

The word “remaining” prevents expenses already included elsewhere from being deducted twice.

Operating profit

The operating result after the costs included in the business’s operating calculation.

Calculation used in this series: Contribution after marketing − remaining operating expenses.

The figure is complete only to the extent that the relevant operating costs are included.

Operating margin

Operating profit expressed as a percentage of net revenue.

Calculation: Operating profit ÷ net revenue × 100.

Read the percentage alongside operating profit dollars. Profit can increase while its margin declines.

Net profit

The final profit after the applicable operating costs, non-operating items and tax.

A dashboard that does not include those items should not label an operating subtotal as net profit.

Net profit also differs from the change in cash during the period.

Contribution per order

The named contribution amount divided by its corresponding order count.

Calculation: Contribution for the defined order population ÷ orders in that population.

State whether the contribution is before or after marketing. A blended contribution per order is not automatically representative of first-order customer economics.

Marketing efficiency and additional spending

Marketing efficiency ratio

Business revenue relative to paid media spend. Commonly abbreviated to MER and displayed as Marketing return on the prototype dashboard.

Calculation used in this series: Net revenue ÷ total paid media spend.

A MER of 6.00 means six dollars of business net revenue for each dollar of paid media spend.

It does not establish that paid media caused all that revenue. A calculation using total marketing costs has a different denominator and should be labelled accordingly.

Return on ad spend

Attributed conversion value relative to the corresponding advertising spend. Commonly abbreviated to ROAS.

Calculation: Attributed conversion value ÷ associated advertising spend.

Its meaning depends on conversion settings, attribution rules, value definitions and reporting dates.

ROAS is not a measure of net profit.

Attributed revenue

Revenue credited to a channel, campaign or interaction under a specified attribution method.

Different platforms can credit the same purchase. Their attributed revenues should not be added together and presented as business revenue.

Attribution also differs from evidence that the channel caused an additional purchase.

Platform conversion

An action counted under an advertising platform’s conversion settings.

It may represent a purchase or another configured action. A reported purchase may come from a new or returning customer.

A platform conversion count is therefore not automatically a new-customer count.

Average return

The return across the activity included in a defined reporting period or population.

Calculation: Total relevant value ÷ total relevant expenditure.

Historical average performance does not establish the return on additional spending.

Marginal return

The return associated with an additional amount of investment or activity.

For an additional-media scenario, the relevant relationship is additional business revenue divided by additional media spend.

When the response is assumed rather than established, label it as a scenario assumption. Do not present it as observed incremental performance.

Incremental revenue

Revenue above the amount the business would otherwise have earned.

Establishing incremental revenue requires evidence about that alternative outcome. A rise in revenue after a spending increase does not, by itself, establish incrementality.

In a scenario, additional revenue may be an explicit assumption rather than a proven effect.

Additional operating profit

The change in operating profit produced by the additional revenue and costs included in a scenario or supported analysis.

Calculation: Additional revenue − associated product and variable costs − additional marketing costs − additional operating costs.

Costs included in a contribution rate must not also be deducted separately.

Break-even requirement

The result required for an activity to cover the costs included in its calculation.

For an additional-spend scenario:

Required additional revenue = additional costs to recover ÷ contribution rate available to recover them.

The rate must be positive and use the correct cost basis. A business-revenue requirement is not automatically equivalent to a platform ROAS target.

Customers, acquisition and value

First-time customer

A customer making their first purchase from the business, based on the available purchase history.

Incomplete history or duplicate identity records can affect the count. State the limits of the customer definition.

Returning customer

A customer who purchased before the reporting period and purchases again within it, under the report’s stated definition.

This series uses a mutually exclusive period classification. A customer first acquired and purchasing again within the period remains in its new-customer group. Their later order is still a repeat order. Source reports may classify returning behaviour differently.

Returning customers can place multiple orders. Their number differs from the number of returning-customer orders.

Customer mix

The composition of the customer population or its sales, such as new versus returning customers.

State whether the measure describes customers, orders or revenue. Each can have a different proportion.

Changes in mix can alter an overall result even when performance within each group remains unchanged.

Business media CAC

Paid media spend relative to first-time customers purchasing during the same reporting period. CAC stands for customer acquisition cost.

Calculation: Period paid media spend ÷ period first-time customers.

The prototype dashboard uses the short label New customer cost.

This is a blended period measure. Spending may support retention, and some first-time customers may arrive through unpaid channels or earlier activity.

Cohort acquisition CAC

Acquisition costs allocated to a defined customer cohort, divided by its acquired customers.

Calculation: Acquisition costs allocated to the cohort ÷ original acquired customers in the cohort.

State the allocation method and included costs. Media-only cohort CAC and fully loaded cohort CAC are different measures.

Fully loaded acquisition CAC

Acquisition cost including the additional acquisition expenses specified in the definition, beyond media alone.

Calculation: Included acquisition expenditure ÷ matching acquired customers.

The numerator may include allocated agency, production or other acquisition costs. Its affordability comparison must use a compatible cost scope.

Cost per order

The specified expenditure divided by its corresponding order count.

Calculation: Included cost ÷ included orders.

It is not customer acquisition cost unless the order population genuinely corresponds to the acquired-customer population being measured.

Allowable CAC

The acquisition amount the business can support under specified contribution, cost and profit requirements.

For the first-order media allowance used in this series:

Allowable media CAC = first-order contribution before media − overhead and other marketing allowance − desired first-order profit.

It is a calculated allowance, not an observed acquisition cost. A negative result means the stated requirement is infeasible under those assumptions.

Cohort

A defined group whose performance is followed over time.

An acquisition-month cohort groups customers by the month of their first purchase. Other cohort definitions are possible, but must remain explicit.

For customer-value analysis, retain the original acquired population, including customers who do not repeat.

Cohort age

The elapsed time since the event defining the cohort, such as each customer’s first purchase.

Compare customer value at equivalent ages. A younger cohort has had less opportunity to generate repeat purchases.

Revenue lifetime value

Cumulative revenue per acquired customer over a specified horizon. Often called revenue LTV.

Calculation: Cohort revenue through the stated age ÷ original acquired customers in the cohort.

Always identify the horizon and whether the figure is observed or forecast. Revenue LTV does not deduct the costs of producing that revenue.

Gross-profit lifetime value

Cumulative gross profit per acquired customer over a specified horizon.

Calculation: Cohort gross profit through the stated age ÷ original acquired customers.

It excludes costs outside the gross-profit definition, so it should not be treated as contribution or net profit.

Contribution lifetime value

Cumulative contribution per acquired customer over a specified horizon.

Calculation: Cohort contribution through the stated age ÷ original acquired customers.

In this series, it includes applicable product, fulfilment, transaction and retention costs, but excludes the initial acquisition media cost being assessed separately.

The cost basis must accompany the metric.

Repeat contribution

Contribution from purchases after the first purchase, over the stated horizon.

Include the applicable costs of serving and retaining those customers.

Repeat contribution is not the same as total contribution from the cohort, which also includes first purchases.

Acquisition payback

The time required for cumulative customer contribution to recover the specified acquisition cost.

Assessment: Identify when cumulative contribution before the initial acquisition cost reaches that cost, using the same cohort and compatible cost definitions.

Report observation maturity and any subsequent reversals. If recovery has not occurred, state that the cohort has not yet paid back within the observed period.

LTV to CAC ratio

The named customer-value measure divided by the corresponding acquisition cost per customer.

Calculation: Specified LTV ÷ matching CAC.

Revenue LTV and contribution LTV produce different interpretations. The ratio also needs a time horizon and does not replace an assessment of payback or cash requirements.

Returning-customer share

The proportion of a defined period’s purchasing customers who meet the returning-customer definition.

Calculation: Returning purchasing customers ÷ all purchasing customers in the period.

Use the period classification above. Do not move newly acquired customers into this group simply because they purchase again within the same reporting period.

This describes period mix. It is not the same as the proportion of an original cohort that subsequently purchases again.

Cash and inventory

Cash balance

Cash held at a specified point in time under the reporting scope.

A cash balance does not show all future commitments. Its availability depends on obligations, restrictions and the business’s reserve requirements.

Customer receipts

Cash received from customers or settled through payment providers during the stated period.

Receipt timing can differ from the date on which a sale appears in a revenue report.

Inventory purchase

An acquisition of stock that may be sold during the current period or later.

The associated cash payment follows the supplier’s terms. Inventory purchases and COGS therefore measure different things.

Inventory payment

A cash payment associated with inventory, including applicable deposits or supplier balances.

Include it on its expected payment date in the cash forecast. Do not deduct accounting COGS again as a second cash payment.

Cash forecast

A dated estimate of cash receipts, payments and resulting balances.

Calculation: Opening cash + cash inflows − cash outflows = closing cash.

Include all material cash flows under the forecast’s scope. Expected financing should not be treated as confirmed cash unless its availability is established.

Forecast cash low point

The lowest projected cash balance within the forecast’s horizon and time resolution.

A weekly closing forecast can miss a lower balance within a week. Use sufficient detail when payment timing is tight.

Required reserve

The minimum cash balance the business intends to preserve.

It reflects the business’s obligations, uncertainty and operating requirements. It is a policy input, not a universal benchmark.

Forecast cash headroom

The amount by which the lowest forecast cash balance exceeds the required reserve.

Calculation: Lowest forecast cash balance − required reserve.

A negative result identifies a projected reserve shortfall. Positive headroom is conditional on the forecast, not automatically unrestricted money available to spend.

Available stock

Units available to satisfy demand under the stated stock policy.

Clarify the treatment of reserved, damaged, committed or otherwise unavailable units. Total physical stock may differ from available stock.

Sales velocity

The rate at which units sell over a defined period.

Calculation: Units sold ÷ elapsed time.

Observed sales can understate demand when stock availability limits purchases.

Expected weekly demand

The number of units expected to be required each week over the relevant planning period.

It may differ from recent average sales because of seasonality, promotions, stockouts or other expected changes. State the assumption.

Weeks of stock cover

The length of time available stock is expected to last at the stated demand rate.

Calculation: Available units ÷ expected weekly unit demand.

Use matching units. Inventory dollars divided by unit demand does not produce weeks of cover.

Replenishment lead time

The elapsed time from the relevant ordering decision until stock becomes available for sale.

Include applicable production, transit, receiving and preparation time. Lead time differs from the remaining time until an existing order arrives.

Confirmed inbound stock

Stock already ordered with a stated quantity and expected arrival.

Keep the arrival assumption visible. Ordered stock is not yet available stock, and expected arrival is not necessarily a guaranteed date.

Potential stock gap

A period in which expected demand exceeds available supply before replenishment becomes sellable.

The estimate depends on stock, demand and arrival assumptions. It does not directly establish lost revenue or lost profit.

Comparisons and interpretation

Previous-period comparison

A comparison with the immediately preceding reporting period.

It helps identify recent movement. Check differences in duration, trading conditions, promotions and data completeness.

Year-on-year comparison

A comparison with the corresponding period in the previous year.

It provides seasonal context, but may still require adjustment or explanation for changed promotional timing, product availability and business conditions.

Month-to-date

The elapsed portion of a month through the stated reporting date.

Compare it with equivalent coverage where appropriate. Label any comparison with a complete month clearly.

Trailing twelve months

The twelve-month period ending at the specified reporting date.

It provides a broader annual view but can conceal recent changes. State whether the calculation uses complete months or another date convention.

Absolute change

The difference between the current and comparison values, expressed in their original unit.

Calculation: Current value − comparison value.

For revenue or profit, this is a dollar change.

Relative change

The change expressed as a proportion of the comparison value.

Calculation: (Current value ÷ comparison value) − 1.

A zero comparison value makes this calculation undefined. Movements involving negative baselines often need a plain explanation of the absolute change.

Percentage-point change

The difference between two rates expressed as percentages.

A change from 2% to 3% is an increase of one percentage point. Its relative increase is 50%.

Label the two forms of change distinctly.

Combined-period ratio

A ratio recalculated from the underlying totals for the combined period.

Calculation: Total relevant numerator ÷ total relevant denominator.

For example, combined operating margin is total operating profit divided by total revenue. It is not generally the simple average of monthly margins.

Mix effect

A change in an aggregate result caused by changes in the proportions of its component groups.

Product, customer, market or traffic mix can change an overall result even when each group’s performance is unchanged.

Baseline

An observed reference used for comparison.

A baseline describes what occurred. It is not automatically an appropriate target.

Benchmark

A documented comparison reference.

Its usefulness depends on matching definitions, populations, periods and business conditions. A benchmark does not establish the cause of a performance difference.

Target

An intended result or operating requirement.

Its basis should be explicit, such as business economics, capacity, historical evidence or an approved objective.

Observation

A recorded result for a specified source, population and period.

An observation establishes what was measured. It does not automatically explain why it occurred.

Scenario

A calculation showing what follows if stated assumptions hold.

A scenario is useful for examining possibilities. Correct arithmetic does not establish that the assumed response will occur.

Forecast

An estimate of a future result based on stated evidence and assumptions.

Keep forecasts separate from observed results and update their interpretation when assumptions change.

Causal claim

A statement that an activity produced an outcome that would not otherwise have occurred.

It requires evidence about the alternative outcome. A before-and-after change or an attributed conversion is not sufficient on its own.

Provisional value

A value that may change as data matures, costs are confirmed or records are reconciled.

Keep provisional values identifiable. A precise display does not imply a completed measurement.

Missing value

An input or result that is unavailable.

Missing is not zero. Where a required input is absent, dependent calculations should remain unavailable or explicitly estimated.
