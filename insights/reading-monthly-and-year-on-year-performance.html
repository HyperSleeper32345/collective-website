Reading monthly and year-on-year performance

By Collective

A performance figure needs a suitable comparison. Revenue can fall from one month to the next while improving against the same month last year. Profit can increase while its margin declines.

These results are not contradictory. They answer different questions.

Reading performance well means choosing the comparison deliberately, keeping the calculation consistent and understanding what the change does and does not establish.

Choose the comparison for the question

A previous-period comparison shows recent movement. A year-on-year comparison provides context from the corresponding period in the previous year.

Both are useful, but neither explains the result on its own.

ComparisonWhat it helps you examineWhat still needs consideration
Previous monthRecent changes in revenue, costs and profitDifferent month lengths, promotions, stock availability and seasonality
Same month last yearPerformance against a comparable point in the trading yearChanges in the business, promotional dates, product range and cost structure
Recent three monthsWhether a pattern extends beyond one monthSeasonal changes and differences within the period
Trailing twelve monthsThe broader annual resultRecent changes that an annual total or average may conceal

Start with the period relevant to the decision. Then use another comparison to test whether the first reading is incomplete.

For an emerging change in fulfilment costs, recent months may be especially useful. For a seasonal sales period, the equivalent prior-year period may provide better context.

The comparison should follow the question.

Read the same result from more than one reference

Consider Example Commerce, the illustrative business introduced in the previous article. All figures below are in Australian dollars and exclude GST.

MeasureOctober 2025September 2026October 2026
Net revenue$180,000$240,000$270,000
Gross margin54.0%55.0%55.0%
Contribution after marketing$47,200$64,000$67,500
Operating profit$24,200$38,000$40,500
Operating margin13.44%15.83%15.00%

Against September, October revenue increased by $30,000 and operating profit increased by $2,500. Operating margin fell from 15.83% to 15.00%.

Against the previous October, revenue increased by $90,000 and operating profit increased by $16,300. Operating margin rose from 13.44% to 15.00%.

The recent comparison shows that profit grew more slowly than revenue. The annual comparison shows a stronger operating result than the corresponding month last year.

Both observations belong in the executive reading. Selecting only one would leave out useful context.

Neither comparison establishes why performance changed. That requires examining the underlying costs, customer behaviour, channel activity and trading conditions.

Compare equivalent coverage

Before interpreting a change, check that the periods contain comparable data.

A month-to-date result should usually be compared with the equivalent elapsed period, rather than a complete month. If the current report covers 1 to 15 October, its comparison should make that coverage clear.

Even complete months differ in length. September has 30 days and October has 31. Total revenue answers how much the business earned in each month. Revenue per day answers a different question about the average pace of sales.

For Example Commerce:

MeasureSeptember 2026October 2026
Net revenue$240,000$270,000
Days in month3031
Average revenue per day$8,000Approximately $8,710

Monthly revenue increased by 12.5%. Average daily revenue increased by approximately 8.9%.

The extra day accounts for part of the difference between those two readings. It does not explain the remaining change.

Daily averages are useful context, but they do not make every period equivalent. Weekends, promotions, public holidays and stock availability may affect the distribution of sales.

Also check whether the figures are complete. Advertising attribution may continue to change after a period ends. Refunds and late cost entries can change the reported commercial result.

A comparison between a mature historical period and an incomplete current period should identify that difference.

Account for seasonality and promotions

A year-on-year comparison helps place a result in its seasonal context. It does not automatically control for every seasonal difference.

A promotion may start earlier, run longer or use a different offer. A major trading event may fall on different dates. One year may have a stockout that the other does not.

Where an event materially affects performance, examine both the calendar period and the event itself.

For a promotion, useful context includes:

  • The dates and duration.
  • The products available.
  • The discount or offer.
  • Media and other marketing investment.
  • Revenue, contribution and profit.
  • Any material stock or fulfilment constraints.

Keep the calendar comparison because it remains part of the business’s monthly result. Use the event comparison to understand the activity within it.

A successful promotion should not be judged on revenue alone. A larger discount may increase sales while leaving less contribution. A promotion can also change when customers purchase, which affects how the surrounding periods should be interpreted.

These are questions to investigate. A spike during the promotion does not, by itself, establish how much additional business it created.

Distinguish dollars, percentages and percentage points

Different measures of change answer different questions.

Absolute change shows the difference in the original unit. For revenue or profit, that means dollars.

Relative change expresses the difference as a proportion of the comparison value.

Percentage-point change describes the difference between two rates expressed as percentages.

Example Commerce’s September-to-October results illustrate all three:

MeasureCalculationChange
Revenue dollars$270,000 − $240,000+$30,000
Revenue growth$30,000 ÷ $240,000+12.5%
Operating profit dollars$40,500 − $38,000+$2,500
Operating profit growth$2,500 ÷ $38,000Approximately +6.6%
Operating margin15.00% − 15.83%Approximately −0.83 percentage points

The margin did not fall by 0.83% in relative terms. It fell by approximately 0.83 percentage points.

That distinction matters when reading conversion rates, margins and customer shares. A move from 2% to 3% is a one-percentage-point increase and a 50% relative increase.

A dashboard should identify which calculation it displays.

When the comparison value is zero, relative percentage change is undefined. Show the absolute change instead. When moving from a loss to a profit, state the dollar improvement and the change in position clearly.

Calculate combined ratios from the underlying totals

Monthly ratios are useful. Combining them requires care.

To calculate a period’s overall margin, divide total profit by total revenue. To calculate overall average order value, divide total revenue by total orders. To calculate MER, divide total business revenue by total paid media spend.

An arithmetic average of monthly ratios gives each month equal weight, regardless of its size. That may answer a specific question about an average month, but it does not necessarily describe the combined period.

For Example Commerce, September and October together produce:

  • Revenue of $510,000.
  • Operating profit of $78,500.

The combined operating margin is:

$78,500 ÷ $510,000 = approximately 15.39%.

The simple average of the two monthly margins is approximately 15.42%.

The difference is small in this example because the months are relatively similar in size. It can be much larger when their revenue differs substantially.

The calculation should follow the measure:

MeasureCombined-period calculation
Operating marginTotal operating profit ÷ total revenue
Average order valueTotal relevant revenue ÷ total relevant orders
MERTotal business revenue ÷ total paid media spend
Purchase-session rateTotal purchasing sessions ÷ total eligible sessions

Keep numerators and denominators on the same basis. Do not combine a rate from one population with a count from another.

Look beneath the aggregate

An overall result can change because individual groups perform differently, because their proportions change, or both.

Consider this separate illustrative example:

Customer groupEarlier period revenueLater period revenueContribution rate before marketing
New customers$50,000$80,00040% in both periods
Returning customers$50,000$20,00050% in both periods
Total$100,000$100,000

In the earlier period, contribution before marketing is $45,000. In the later period, it is $42,000.

Total revenue is unchanged. Each group’s contribution rate is unchanged. The overall contribution rate falls from 45% to 42% because more revenue comes from the lower-contribution group.

The change in customer mix explains the arithmetic. It does not establish whether the new mix is commercially undesirable. The acquired customers may contribute again later, and acquisition costs also need examination.

The same principle applies elsewhere:

  • Product mix can change gross margin.
  • Traffic mix can change the overall conversion rate.
  • New and returning customer mix can change average order value.
  • Market mix can change fulfilment costs.

Before concluding that performance deteriorated within a group, inspect both the group’s result and its share of the total.

Keep definitions consistent across periods

A comparison can look precise while measuring different things.

One period may include shipping income while another excludes it. Product costs may have been updated. Agency fees may move between reporting categories. Tracking changes may alter the number of recorded purchases.

A real change in costs should appear in performance. A change in classification or measurement should be explained separately.

Check that the periods use consistent:

  • Revenue and tax treatment.
  • Cost inclusions.
  • Order and customer definitions.
  • Attribution settings where relevant.
  • Currency and timezone.
  • Treatment of refunds and adjustments.

If a definition changes, preserve the explanation. Recalculate comparable history where reliable inputs allow it, or identify the break in the series.

Do not interpret a reporting change as a commercial improvement.

What to examine on the dashboard

Read the selected period against its stated comparison, then examine the trend around it.

Start with the absolute revenue and profit changes. Check the margins next. This establishes whether the business is producing more profit and how much revenue it requires to do so.

Then ask:

  • Does the prior-year comparison support the same interpretation?
  • Are both periods complete and measured consistently?
  • Did promotions, stock availability or trading conditions differ?
  • Did product, customer or channel mix change?
  • Which underlying cost or performance measure accounts for the movement?

Use recent periods to identify emerging changes and longer periods to understand context. Neither should automatically override the other.

The outcome should be a precise observation: what changed, against which reference, and what needs investigation next. That provides a sound starting point for examining marketing efficiency, acquisition economics and growth.
